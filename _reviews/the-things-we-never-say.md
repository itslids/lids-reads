This one was quite good, surprisingly easy for a Booker finalist. I didn't find this to be particularly challenging, either in the way it was written or the message it contained.

I found the political commentary kind of… gimmicky. I don't know if that's the right word, but it rang hollow, like, "every writer must be a little leftist and therefore this book will be, too." But without real conviction. Some of it was just almost crossing into preachy territory, and this almost made me take off another half star.

And yet, even with these little nits I have, I still found it to be superbly written and poignant in its simplest moments: Artie's awe at certain things, like the delight of his students, and his daughter-in-law's musical talent and focus. Artie's evolving relationship with his son, his son's development into something like the next iteration of Artie himself.

I think I'd have loved it if this book explored more about what it means to be a contemporary American man and less about hollow, vague political fears. Overall, a sort of hearty, simple book that, it wouldn't surprise me, may be a glimpse at this very specific moment in American history someday.
